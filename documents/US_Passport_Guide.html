Your Passport Guide

For our American friends and family — everything you need to make sure your passport is ready in time. Most of you already have one. Some of you may need to renew. A few may be applying for the first time. We've got you covered.

I.The most important rule

Most US travellers get caught off guard by this single requirement.

To enter Portugal — and any country in the Schengen Area — your passport must meet two conditions:

For Our Wedding
Your passport must be valid through at least November 2028

and must have been issued sometime within the last 10 years.

The legal Schengen requirement is that your passport be valid for at least three months after you leave Europe. But many airlines enforce a stricter six-month rule using their own automated systems — meaning a passport with four months of validity might be accepted at the border but get you turned away at the gate before you ever board.

Don't gamble on it. If your passport expires any time in 2028, renew now. The fee is the same whether you renew in May 2027 or May 2028, and the new passport is good for ten more years.

Pull out your passport right now and check the expiration date. It's printed on the photo page.

II.Where you stand

Find yourself in one of these four situations, then jump to that section.

Path A

My passport is valid through November 2028 or later. You are all set — nothing to do. Skim the rest of this guide if you'd like, but the work is already done. We'll see you in Portugal.

Path B

I have a passport but it expires before November 2028. You need to renew. The good news: most adults can do this online or by mail without leaving home. Skip to the renewal section.

Path C

I have never had a US passport. You'll apply in person at a post office or other acceptance facility. It's straightforward, just a few documents. Skip to the first-time application section.

Path D

My child needs a passport. Children under 16 have their own process — both parents typically need to be present, and there's a separate form. Skip to the children's section.

Most of you will fall into Path A and can put this guide aside. If you're not sure which path applies — for instance, if you can't find your passport, or if it's water-damaged or has missing pages — treat it as Path C and apply for a new one. Damaged passports cannot be renewed.

III.When to apply

Processing can take eight to ten weeks door-to-door, longer at peak times.

The State Department's current processing times are 4-6 weeks for routine service and 2-3 weeks for expedited service. Add about two weeks for mailing in each direction, and the realistic door-to-door window is 8-10 weeks routine, 6-7 weeks expedited. Spring and summer are peak season, so build in extra cushion.

Now – Sep 2027The Easy Window

Apply now if you possibly can. Demand is lower in fall and early winter, processing is fastest, and you'll have your passport months before you need it. There's no benefit to waiting.

Oct – Dec 2027Still Comfortable

Routine service is fine. You'll have your passport in plenty of time to apply for ETIAS afterward and book flights with confidence.

Jan – Feb 2028The Last Safe Moment

Pay for expedited service ($60 extra) — peak season has begun and routine times can stretch. Add the $22.05 for 1-3 day return shipping, too.

Mar – Apr 2028Cutting It Close

You'll likely need an in-person appointment at one of the 26 regional passport agencies, which require proof of travel within 14 days. Possible, but stressful. Please don't let it get to this point.

IV.Renewing your passport

For most adults — the path of least resistance.

Are you eligible to renew?

You can renew (rather than reapply from scratch) if all of the following are true:

  • You have your most recent passport in hand
  • It is undamaged (no water damage, no missing or torn pages)
  • It was issued when you were 16 or older
  • It was issued within the last 15 years
  • It is in your current legal name (or you have your name-change paperwork)

If any of those don't apply, you'll need to apply in person as a first-time applicant. Skip to that section.

Two ways to renew: online or by mail

If you're eligible, the State Department now offers online renewal at travel.state.gov. The processing time is the same as mail (4-6 weeks routine), but you save the trouble of printing forms, finding stamps, and a trip to the post office.

If you prefer to renew by mail (or aren't eligible for online), you'll fill out Form DS-82, attach your old passport, a new passport photo, and a check, and mail it in.

1

Get a new passport photo

Most CVS, Walgreens, FedEx, and post offices take passport photos for around $15-20. Specific requirements: 2×2 inches, plain white background, neutral expression, no glasses, taken within the last six months.

For online renewal, you'll upload a digital version of the photo — many post offices and pharmacies offer a digital file alongside the print for a small extra fee.

2

Fill out Form DS-82

If renewing online, the website walks you through it. If by mail, use the form filler at travel.state.gov and print on single-sided paper. Do not double-side print.

3

Pay the fee

$130 for a passport book. Add $60 for expedited service. Add $22.05 for fast 1-3 day return shipping. Most people will want both add-ons if applying after January 2028.

For mail renewals: write a check or money order payable to "U.S. Department of State." Online renewals accept credit cards.

4

Submit and wait

Mail-in renewals go to the address printed on Form DS-82 (it varies by region). Use trackable shipping. Online renewals submit themselves — you'll mail your old passport separately when prompted.

Your old passport will be returned to you in a separate envelope a few weeks after your new one arrives, hole-punched and marked "void." Keep it as a souvenir — old passport stamps are wonderful keepsakes.

V.Applying for the first time

A bit more involved, but still very manageable. You'll need an in-person appointment.

First-time adult applicants and anyone who can't renew by mail must apply in person at an authorized passport acceptance facility. These are typically post offices, public libraries, or county clerk's offices — there are over 7,000 across the country. You'll find your nearest one at iafdb.travel.state.gov.

1

Gather your documents

You'll need:

  • Proof of US citizenship — a certified birth certificate (the one with the raised seal, not a photocopy) or a Certificate of Naturalization. Digital or laminated birth certificates are not accepted.
  • Photo ID — a valid driver's license or government ID
  • Photocopy of the front and back of your photo ID, on plain white paper
  • One passport photo (2×2 inches, white background)
  • Payment for fees (more on this below)
2

Fill out Form DS-11 — but don't sign it yet

Use the online form filler at travel.state.gov to complete Form DS-11, then print it on single-sided paper. Do not sign the form in advance. A passport agent must witness your signature in person at the appointment, and pre-signed forms have to be redone on the spot.

3

Make an appointment

Most acceptance facilities require an appointment. Post offices use the USPS scheduler at tools.usps.com/rcas.htm. Some libraries and government offices use their own systems. Walk-ins are sometimes accepted but never guaranteed.

4

Attend the appointment

Bring all your documents and your unsigned form. The agent will verify your ID, watch you sign the form, take your oath, and seal everything in an envelope to mail to the State Department. The appointment usually takes 15-30 minutes.

5

Pay two separate fees

This is the only step that confuses people. There are two fees:

  • $130 application fee to the State Department — paid by check or money order made out to "U.S. Department of State"
  • $35 acceptance fee to the facility — paid separately. Most post offices accept cards, others only checks. Confirm at booking.

Add $60 for expedited service and $22.05 for 1-3 day return shipping if you want them.

6

Wait, and track

Your birth certificate will be mailed back separately, two to four weeks after your new passport arrives. Track your application's status at travel.state.gov.

VI.Children under 16

There's no shortcut here — every child needs to apply in person, every time.

Children's passports are only valid for five years (not ten like adults), and they cannot be renewed. Each time a child's passport expires, you submit a brand-new application from scratch. This is true whether your child is three or fifteen.

Critical: both parents must consent

For any child under 16, the State Department requires both parents (or legal guardians) to authorize the passport. The simplest way is to have both parents present at the appointment, with the child.

If only one parent can attend, the absent parent must complete Form DS-3053 (Statement of Consent), get it notarized, and the attending parent brings it along with a photocopy of the absent parent's ID. The notarized form must be dated within three months of the appointment.

1

Gather documents

  • The child's certified birth certificate — listing both parents' full names, with raised seal
  • Both parents' photo IDs (driver's licenses or passports)
  • Photocopies of both parents' IDs (front and back)
  • One passport photo of the child (2×2, white background)
  • Form DS-11 — completed but unsigned
  • Form DS-3053, notarized — only if one parent cannot attend
2

The passport photo for young children

Photos of infants and toddlers are notoriously tricky — eyes must be open, mouth closed, no other people visible (no parent's hand holding their head). Most pharmacies have practice handling this, but it can take several attempts. Lay babies on a plain white sheet to photograph from above. Older children sit normally.

3

Bring the child to the appointment

Yes — even infants must appear in person. The agent will verify the child against the photo and the birth certificate. The child does not need to "do" anything; they just need to be there.

4

Pay the fees

Children under 16 pay $100 to the State Department (not $130) plus the same $35 acceptance fee. Total: $135 per child. Expedited service and fast shipping cost the same as for adults.

For families bringing the three-year-old and eight-year-old we know are coming: we strongly recommend applying by November 2027 at the latest. Children's passport photos are the most likely cause of delays, and you'll want time to redo them if needed.

VIII.A few things to watch for

Stick to the official site

The only legitimate place to apply or renew is travel.state.gov. There are dozens of look-alike websites that charge $50-$150 in unnecessary "service fees" to fill out the form for you — often errors and all. The forms themselves are free, the State Department fees are fixed, and the process is genuinely manageable on your own.

If a search result looks like the State Department but the address doesn't end in .gov, close it.

A few other tips that will save you headaches:

  • Don't laminate your new passport when it arrives — it voids it.
  • Don't sign Form DS-11 in advance; it must be signed in front of the agent.
  • Make photocopies of every document you submit, plus your new passport once you have it. Keep one set at home and one digitally accessible.
  • Sign your new passport in ink as soon as it arrives — an unsigned passport is technically invalid.
  • Once you have your passport, apply for ETIAS (the EU's new pre-travel authorisation). We've sent a separate guide for that.

IX.Common questions

My passport expires in October 2028. Do I really need to renew before the wedding?

Yes. By the time of the wedding in May 2028, you'd have only five months of validity remaining — under the airline's six-month rule. Renew now while it's easy.

Can I just bring my driver's license to Portugal?

No. A US driver's license is not a valid travel document for international flights. You absolutely need a passport book. (A passport card works only for land and sea entry from Canada, Mexico, and the Caribbean — not for Europe.)

How long is my new passport valid?

Ten years for adults (issued at age 16 or older). Five years for children. The expiration date is printed on the photo page.

My name has changed since my last passport. What do I do?

If the change happened within the last year, use Form DS-5504 (free correction). If it's been longer, use the regular renewal forms but include a certified copy of your marriage certificate, divorce decree, or court order documenting the name change.

I lost my passport. Now what?

Report it lost via Form DS-64 at travel.state.gov (or by phone), then apply for a replacement in person using Form DS-11 — you cannot renew by mail in this situation. Your old passport will be invalidated immediately, so don't report it lost until you're sure it's truly gone.

Should I get a passport card too?

Probably not, unless you cross into Canada or Mexico by land or sea regularly. Passport cards do not work for international flights — you'd be paying $30 extra for something you can't use to fly to Portugal.

My child has my last name but my spouse and I have different last names. Will that cause issues?

No, as long as the birth certificate lists both parents' full legal names and both parents are present (or properly consenting via DS-3053). The agent doesn't care that your names differ — only that the documentation matches.

What if I run into trouble or have questions the agent can't answer?

The National Passport Information Center is open weekdays 8am-10pm Eastern, weekends 10am-3pm: 1-877-487-2778. They're surprisingly helpful and patient.
